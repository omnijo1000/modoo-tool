Why Google Rewrites Your Meta Description

Guide · Last verified Aug 28, 2026

You've probably carefully written a meta description, only to see Google's search results show an entirely different sentence. That's not a bug. Google treats your meta description as more of a "suggestion" — and in practice, it frequently pulls a sentence it judges more relevant straight from your page copy and shows that instead. This guide walks through exactly what role meta descriptions actually play in SEO (and what role they don't), when Google overrides them, and why you should keep writing them anyway.

1. Meta descriptions are not a ranking factor — straight from Google

Google has stated repeatedly and officially since 2009 that meta descriptions (and meta keywords) are not used in calculating search rankings. In other words, no matter how perfectly you write your meta description, it won't move your page's ranking by even one position on its own. On this point, meta descriptions sit in a fundamentally different category from the title tag or the quality of your body content.

2. So why keep writing them? The indirect path through CTR

The fact that meta descriptions don't directly affect ranking is a different claim from "they don't matter for SEO." The copy that decides whether a user actually clicks on a search result is the meta description — or more precisely, the snippet Google displays. At the same rank and with the same title, a more compelling description lifts click-through rate (CTR), and CTR combined with user engagement signals is believed to indirectly influence ranking over the long run. So the real job of a meta description isn't "raise the ranking" — it's "win the click at the spot you've already earned."

3. Why Google ignores your meta description and rewrites it

Google tries to make the snippet reflect the relevance between the search query and the page as closely as possible. When the words a user searched for aren't present in your written meta description, or when Google judges the description to be a poor match for search intent, it instead extracts a sentence directly from your page's body content that relates to the query and shows that as the snippet. This isn't some rare edge case limited to a handful of pages — large-scale SEO industry studies have repeatedly confirmed it as common behavior. The more specific or long-tail the query, the more often the body excerpt gets shown instead of the description you wrote.

Key takeaway: it's more accurate to think of your meta description not as a guaranteed snippet, but as a "recommendation" you're submitting to Google. Whether Google actually adopts that recommendation is decided fresh for every individual query.

4. Why it's still worth writing a good one

  • It raises the odds Google adopts it: a description that matches search intent and accurately summarizes the page is more likely to be chosen over a body excerpt.
  • It's the fallback humans actually see: when Google does adopt it, that exact wording is what shows in the results and decides whether someone clicks.
  • Keyword stuffing backfires: a description that's just a list of keywords unrelated to natural phrasing actually raises the odds Google judges it "low relevance" and ignores it. Natural sentences aligned with search intent get adopted more often.

5. Where the "150-160 characters" rule actually comes from

The "keep it under 150-160 characters" guideline that shows up in nearly every meta description write-up isn't actually a fixed technical limit. Google truncates snippets in its search results UI based on pixel width, not character count (roughly 680px on mobile, 920px on desktop). Converted to Latin-alphabet text, that width tends to cut off around the 150-160 character mark, which is how that number became the conventional guideline — but for a language like Korean, where each character occupies much more horizontal width, the same pixel width only fits about 70-80 characters. There's no single correct answer to "how many characters," in other words — the real rule is "don't get cut off within this width."

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Does not having a meta description hurt my ranking?

A. No. It's not a ranking factor, so leaving it out won't lower your ranking. But without one, Google will always fall back to a snippet pulled from your page copy.

Q. Does putting keywords in the meta description help ranking?

A. There's no direct ranking benefit. But if a word matches the search query, Google bolds it in the snippet, which can make your listing stand out visually among the other results and lift click-through rate.

Q. Does Google always pull from the page copy, or only sometimes?

A. It depends on how relevant your written meta description is to the query. When relevance is high, Google tends to use it as-is; the more specific or question-like the query, the more likely Google is to swap in a more precisely matching sentence from the body text instead.

Q. Can I write one meta description that fits every possible search query?

A. No meta description can perfectly cover every query. Your best bet is a description that clearly summarizes the page's core topic and value — that maximizes the odds Google adopts it for the queries that matter.
